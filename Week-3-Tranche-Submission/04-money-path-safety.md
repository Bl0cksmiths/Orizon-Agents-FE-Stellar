# 04 — The money path, and how it is made safe (Epic 4)

**Why it matters:** every other route in this service either moves a buyer's own money under an authorization the buyer signed on-chain, or moves nothing at all. The refund path is the first that spends **the platform's own settler balance**, on a human adjudicator's say-so, with nothing on-chain to bound the recipient, the amount, or the fact of it. Its worst failure is not a 500 — it is paying a buyer twice out of a wallet that has no undo, or leaving a buyer who was told their claim stood unable ever to be paid. Deliverable D3 is only worth having if that path is safe, so this document is the part of D3 a reviewer should audit hardest.

**Status: built, merged and hardened; not yet switched on, on the deployment.** Epic 4 ([BLO-8](https://linear.app/bl0cksmiths/issue/BLO-8)) — stories 4.02 ([BLO-30](https://linear.app/bl0cksmiths/issue/BLO-30)), 4.03 ([BLO-31](https://linear.app/bl0cksmiths/issue/BLO-31)), 4.04 ([BLO-32](https://linear.app/bl0cksmiths/issue/BLO-32)), 4.05 ([BLO-33](https://linear.app/bl0cksmiths/issue/BLO-33)) and 4.06 ([BLO-34](https://linear.app/bl0cksmiths/issue/BLO-34)) all Done, followed by a dedicated hardening pass merged as [BE #75](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-BE-Stellar/pull/75) and [FE #76](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-FE-Stellar/pull/76) on 2026-09-25. Testnet only (SOW §3.6).

The switch is checked before the adjudicator guard, so the route refuses every caller identically whatever key they send — which also means an outside reviewer cannot tell from the response whether `API_KEY` is set, and neither can we without the Render dashboard. | `POST https://orizon-agents-be-stellar.onrender.com/api/disputes/dsp_0000000000000000/uphold` with `{}` answers **`503`** and `{"code":"dispute_refunds_disabled","message":"dispute refunds disabled"}`. `/reject` with `{"note":"test"}` answers the same. | **The deployment still runs a build that predates the hardening merge.** | The fixes described in part two are on `main` and are not serving traffic. A third condition compounds both, and QA logged it as one of the week's two Criticals: no finished run on the deployment has a settlement record, so on the deployment no step can be disputed either — **D-050** ([BE #67](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-BE-Stellar/issues/67), Critical, open), which is D-039's consequence rather than a defect in the dispute code. The cause is at the contract level: `PaymentEscrow.charge` cannot move a buyer's funds ([Smart-Contract #3](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-Smart-Contract-Stellar/issues/3), D-039), so the charge never lands, and `_record_settlement` returns early without a `job_id`. Together these are why **none of the defects in part two was ever reachable by a user** — and also why D3's on-chain evidence run is still outstanding. See [`03`](./03-deliverable-D3-dispute-refund.md).

Neither of the two gaps in the table is a development task: turning the switch on requires `API_KEY` in the Render dashboard, and the process refuses to boot with the switch on and no key. D-050 is different — it needs the escrow charge fixed at the contract level before any of this path can be reached at all.

## Part one — the safety properties as designed

Each property below is a design decision with a recorded reason, not an implementation detail. The right-hand column is where a reviewer checks it against the code on `origin/main` of [Orizon-Agents-BE-Stellar](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-BE-Stellar).

| The property, and how it is enforced | Where to check it |
| --- | --- |
| **The mutex is taken before the signature, not after.** A `refund_claims` row keyed on the dispute (`dispute_id` PRIMARY KEY, `INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING`) is inserted before the transfer is built, in the **same single statement** that moves the dispute to `crediting` — so a crash, a retry, a restart or a second adjudicator cannot produce a second transfer, and cannot leave a claim over a dispute that still reads `upheld`. Concurrency is settled by the primary key, never by the status the statement reads: both CTEs share one snapshot, so a status can rule a claim out and can never arbitrate between two claimants. | ADR 0008 D2; `app/services/dispute_store.py` (the claim statement, ~`:721-735`); `app/services/dispute_svc.py` `uphold` step 7 |
| **A payout whose fate is unknown blocks further attempts rather than releasing them.** `invoke_with_server_key_async` returns `{"status": "timeout", "hash": …}` without raising, and that transaction may still settle. Anything that is not an explicit `FAILED` — a raised exception, a shutdown cancellation, a `SUCCESS` with no hash, an unrecognised status — is treated as unknown: the claim is **held**, the dispute stays `crediting` with the in-flight hash recorded, the event is logged at ERROR with dispute id, job id, payer and amount, and the adjudicator is answered `504 refund_unconfirmed`. A human reconciles from `list_refund_claims()`. Only a `FAILED` result releases the claim and leaves the dispute `upheld` and payable. This trades paying late for never paying twice, and says so. | ADR 0008 D3; `app/services/refund_svc.py`; `docs/disputes.md` § "For operators: reconciling a dispute stuck in `crediting`" |
| **Status transitions are compare-and-set.** A decision made against a stale read is refused rather than applied: `open → upheld` is written with its precondition as a **clause of the same INSERT** (`expected_status`), so there is no window between the check and the write. A caller that loses the set re-reads and is answered with the credit that already exists, or refused `409 adjudication_in_progress` rather than racing the winner to the mutex. The mutex `DELETE` sits behind an `EXISTS` that repeats the same precondition, so it moves only when the trail does. | `app/services/dispute_store.py` (the append statement, ~`:595-647`); `dispute_svc.py` `uphold` step 6 and `reject` (`expected_status="open"`, `:693`) |
| **The amount is clamped three ways, before anything is signed.** `refund_svc.creditable_for` produces `min(dispute.creditable_usdc, step.price_usdc × fraction, settlement.settled_usdc)` over the step the dispute names — the creditable figure frozen when the dispute opened, the step's settled price times the policy fraction in force now, and what the charge actually moved — rounded to the ledger's seven decimals. Every clamp that bites is logged at WARNING with both numbers. None of the three is safe alone: the per-step figure originates as the planner's estimate, so refunding it would mean paying back money never collected; the frozen figure is the promise the buyer was shown, so lowering `DISPUTE_CREDITED_FRACTION` reaches open disputes while raising it cannot. | ADR 0008 D4; `app/services/refund_svc.py:173-303` |
| **A hard ceiling is checked before anything is signed.** `MAX_REFUND_USDC` ships at `1.0` and is deliberately **not** `MAX_CHARGE_USDC` (100.0): one bounds what a buyer authorised themselves to spend, the other bounds what the platform pays out of its own wallet on an adjudicator's say-so. A step on this deployment costs hundredths of a USDC, so `1.0` is far above anything legitimate and still keeps the blast radius of a leaked settler key, a mistaken uphold, or an amount that arrived wrong small. Non-finite amounts are refused ahead of the caps. | ADR 0008 D5; `app/config.py:150, 256`; `refund_svc.py:276, 299, 365, 374` |
| **The adjudication door fails closed.** `require_adjudicator` refuses when `API_KEY` is empty, on **every** network, rather than waving the caller through as `require_api_key` deliberately does for the read-scoped demo. It answers the three cases in the order a caller meets them: switch off → `503 dispute_refunds_disabled`; switch on with no key → `503 adjudication_not_configured`, logged at ERROR; missing or wrong key → `401 invalid_api_key`, with no refusal distinguishing "no key" from "wrong key", because an adjudication endpoint that did would be an oracle. Rejecting is behind the same guard as upholding: a rejection is terminal and cheap, so an open reject route would let anyone dispose of every dispute raised against the platform. | ADR 0008 D1; `app/security.py:445-` (`_operator_key_scheme`, `require_adjudicator`); `app/routers/disputes.py` |
| **The refund path has its own master switch, off by default.** `DISPUTE_REFUNDS_ENABLED=False` means the shipped configuration cannot pay anything at all. Turning it on makes `API_KEY` mandatory at boot on every network including testnet — the process raises a `ConfigurationError` and exits rather than serving an anonymous payout route. The switch is enforced **again** inside `dispute_svc.uphold`, before the store is even read, because the operator script is a second door into the same service and a switch only one door honours is not a switch. | `app/config.py:263, 418`; `dispute_svc.py` `uphold` step 0 |
| **Nothing reads as done until the chain says so.** A transaction hash is recorded on TIMEOUT as well as on SUCCESS, so the presence of a hash proves nothing landed. The record therefore carries an explicit `rating_confirmed`, true only on SUCCESS or on a ledger replay that confirms an earlier attempt, and the hardening made it **monotonic in both stores** so a timed-out re-submission can never replace a hash the ledger has already vouched for. The receipt holds its tick to that flag, and reads an absent field as "not known" rather than as false. | [#65](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-BE-Stellar/pull/65) (the four fields a truthful receipt needs); [#75](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-BE-Stellar/pull/75) § Money; `dispute_store.py:534-548, 1129-1155` |
| **The window cannot be moved after the fact.** `SettlementRecord.window_closes_at` is stamped once, at settlement, from the `DISPUTE_WINDOW_SECONDS` in force at that instant (86,400 s), and every later check compares against that field rather than recomputing `settled_at + config`. Retuning the setting then governs workflows that settle after the change and nothing that already happened — so shortening it cannot close a window the platform promised was open, and lengthening it cannot reopen one an operator was told had closed. Timestamps are epoch seconds from our own clock, never the database's. | ADR 0007 D1; `dispute_store.py:145, 826, 1407` |

**The trust model, stated rather than implied.** The platform funds the credit over the asset SAC (`transfer(settler → buyer)`); it is never a clawback from the agent, whose only consequence is reputational. The platform adjudicates, and there is no on-chain arbitration in this sprint — a permissioned, trusted operation under SOW §3.8. `PaymentEscrow` has no `refund` entrypoint and never takes custody, so a dispute credit cannot be a reversal of the charge; it has to be a new transfer from somewhere else. All of that is in ADR 0002 and repeated in `docs/disputes.md` § "The trust model, stated plainly".

**Design records** (backend repo, `docs/decisions/`): ADR 0002 (the credit mechanism and the trust model), ADR 0007 (the window, the proof of payer and the settlement record), ADR 0008 (who may order a payout and how it is paid exactly once), ADR 0009 (the dispute rating and its replay guard). Buyer- and operator-facing version, including the reconciliation procedure: `docs/disputes.md`.

## Part two — what attacking our own work found

Before declaring Epic 4 done we ran a full audit pass over everything it produced. Two rules made it worth doing. **Nothing counted as a finding until it had been reproduced against the running service** — six read-only audits drove the real backend and two drove the real frontend components against the real hook, and a suspicion that could not be made to happen was discarded. And **every fix began with a test written first and watched failing**, then teeth-checked by reverting the fix to confirm the test goes red. Five backend fix lanes and two frontend lanes followed.

Shipped as [BE #75](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-BE-Stellar/pull/75) (100 commits, 43 files, +5,180/−514) and [FE #76](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-FE-Stellar/pull/76) (39 commits, 16 files, +2,281/−209), both merged 2026-09-25. Those two PR bodies are the authoritative list; the table below is the substance of it.

### The money

| What was wrong | How it showed up | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A settler could pay a dispute twice.** `append_status` was an unconditional append, so two adjudications of one dispute both passed the status check and both signed. | Reproduced against the service: **two transfers, 0.10 USDC out, and a record saying 0.05**. Independently found by QA the day before as D-053 — see part three. | `open → upheld` is now a compare-and-set whose precondition is a clause of the INSERT itself. A caller that finds the dispute already moved re-reads and is answered with the existing credit, or refused `409 adjudication_in_progress`. |
| **A stale decision could strand a live payout's mutex**, leaving a dispute nobody could ever pay. A data-modifying CTE runs whether or not the INSERT beside it writes a row, so the `refund_claims` DELETE fired for transitions the precondition had just refused — a `reject` computed from a stale `open` read dropped the mutex of a transfer that was on the network. | Reproduced through the store, on the fake pool and on a real Postgres. | The DELETE sits behind an `EXISTS` that repeats the precondition, so the mutex moves only when the trail does. |
| **A lost update in the append path.** `FOR UPDATE` inside the reading CTE does not fix it: at READ COMMITTED a statement that waits on a row lock does not re-take its snapshot, so the waiter copies forward the row it read before waiting. | Reproduced by modelling the statement's snapshot window in the store's fake pool, then confirmed on Postgres. | The lock is its own statement in the same transaction, taken on the dispute's **opening** row rather than its newest. |
| **A misconfigured deployment parked a payment mid-flight.** With the refund switch on and no signing key wired, the claim was taken, the transfer raised before any submission, and the raise could not be told from one that may have landed — so the dispute sat in `crediting` with the claim held, telling the operator the transfer "MAY HAVE LANDED — do not retry". Only a database edit freed it. | Reproduced by booting the service in that exact configuration. | The settler configuration is checked **before** the transition: nothing is claimed, the dispute is left exactly as the buyer left it, and the answer is `503 refunds_not_configured` — a different problem from `adjudication_not_configured`, and now documented as such. |
| **`NaN` defeated both refund caps** and then wedged the transfer at `usdc_to_i128`. | Reproduced by feeding a non-finite amount through `creditable_for`. | Non-finite amounts are refused ahead of the caps. (QA's D-054 is the remaining half of this: a non-finite *cap* is still not validated. Still open — see part three.) |
| **A confirmed rating could end up recorded beside a later, unconfirmed hash** — a tick on a transaction that never landed. | Reproduced by re-submitting after a confirmation. | `rating_confirmed` is monotonic in both stores, and a timed-out re-submission no longer replaces a hash the ledger has vouched for. |
| **A charge that times out records no settlement**, so a buyer who may have been charged cannot dispute at all — and the docstrings asserted a guarantee the code does not provide, claiming a missing job id meant no money moved. | Found by reading the settlement path against the charge's three answers. | Logged as an unconfirmed charge naming payer, amount and job id; the false docstrings corrected. The durable fix is a third settlement state, and it was **deliberately not invented** in a hardening pass — it is named in the PR's "Not in this PR". |

### Disclosure

| What was wrong | How it showed up | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The buyer's own words and the platform's rejection reason were readable by a caller who had proved nothing.** `GET /api/tasks` is public, so task ids led to disputes, and `GET /api/disputes/{id}` returned the buyer's verbatim `reason` and the adjudicator's `rejection_reason` with no credential at all. | Reproduced over HTTP from the public task listing, anonymously. | Chain-public facts stay open so shared trace links keep working; the free text now needs a task read token or the operator key. The dispute id was widened to **128 bits**, since it is in practice the sole bearer capability on a buyer's complaint. |
| **Any stranger could deny every buyer their dispute window.** One shared 500-entry challenge table served all three challenge purposes, with an eviction fallback that evicted **live** entries. | Proven over HTTP through the *bind* scope: **510 anonymous mints evicted a buyer's live dispute challenge**. | The budget is partitioned per purpose (150/150/200, the memory bound unchanged), expired entries are swept, and a live entry is never evicted — a mint over budget is refused `503 challenge_capacity_{purpose}`. |
| **A boot failure printed configuration secrets into the deploy log.** A pydantic `ValidationError` carries the settings input dict, truncated head-and-tail, so *which* secrets escaped depended on environment-key ordering. | Reproduced by booting with a deliberately invalid setting and reading what the traceback rendered. | A failed boot raises a clean error built from the validator message and the variable name, with no exception chain left for a reporting tool to walk. (Story 4.03 had already patched the one script that hit this path; the audit closed the general case.) |
| **Four routes reflected caller input into `error.message`**, two of them world-readable while `TASK_AUTH_REQUIRED` is off, as it is in production. | Reproduced by sending crafted ids and reading the envelope back. | The refusals no longer echo caller text; pinned by tests that assert the response carries none. |

### Reputation

| What was wrong | How it showed up | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **One agent used twice lost half its ratings.** Every step was submitted under the run's `job_id`, and `ReputationLedger`'s `Rated(agent_id, job_id)` guard rejects the second as a replay *before* it reads `kind` — the same collision 4.04 had already solved for dispute ratings, still live for ordinary ones. | Reproduced by running a plan with one agent on two steps and reading the ledger's answers. | Ratings derive a per-step id in the shape of ADR 0009 D1 under a distinct tag. **Step 0 keeps the sealed job id**, so nothing already on-chain changes meaning. |
| **Both steps were graded on whichever output landed last.** `delivered` was keyed by agent while its siblings were keyed by plan-step index. | Reproduced through the run loop with one agent on two steps. | All four run-loop maps are keyed by plan-step index — including `undispatched`, which otherwise dropped the rating for a step that delivered and was charged for. |

### The receipt

| What was wrong | How it showed up | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The receipt asserted a transfer existed when none had been submitted.** `refundArtifact` documents that an upheld dispute is "decided but not yet sent: pending, and never a hash". The receipt contradicted that in four places: "the credit **is being sent** to your wallet"; an on-chain caption hardcoded to "what you received" over an empty record; a mark reading "Being sent" with no hash; and a `crediting` sentence claiming "The refund was submitted" while `refund_tx` was null. A buyer reading it went looking on Stellar Expert for a transaction nobody had attempted. | Reproduced by rendering the real component against the real hook in each artifact state. | The copy follows the artifact's state: "has not been sent yet — there is no transaction to look up until the platform submits it"; "what is owed to you" until the transfer confirms; "No transaction on record". Both the unit tests and the e2e now assert the row cannot claim **any** submission without a hash to show for it. |
| **The panel could lie about money, permanently.** "Nothing on this workflow was charged" was terminal — if the second settlement read answered `null`, nothing asked again, no error was set and no retry was offered. A single 404 on any re-read destroyed a live receipt, because the old-backend fallback ran on every read and replaced the held snapshot with an empty one. The clock overstated the window, absorbing the whole round trip, so a 45 s cold-start read rendered it open 15 s after the server had stopped accepting disputes. | Each reproduced against the real hook with only `fetch`, the wallet and the clock faked. | An unsettled-but-sealed run is re-asked on a bounded backoff before the copy asserts anything; the fallback is first-read only; the window is measured from the request and errs shut. |
| **A dispute from a different job was pinned onto this settlement's step**, showing a refund for money that was not refunded on this run and removing that step's Dispute button. **One unrecognised row blanked the whole receipt** and then failed every 5 s for ever. | Both reproduced by constructing the case against the real hook. | A dispute is matched on the job it belongs to; a row this build cannot read costs that row and nothing else. |
| **The receipt and the dialog printed different percentages for the same policy** — 6.3% against 6.25% — and the receipt rounded the buyer's credit **up**. | Reproduced by rendering both surfaces from the same policy fraction. | One `formatCreditShare` serves both and never rounds a share up; `formatUsdc` floors to the stroop, so no figure on a receipt is one stroop larger than the chain can move. |

Further accessibility and consistency findings on the same surfaces — dropped keyboard focus on completing a dispute, a character counter that announced on every keystroke, three links sharing one accessible name, an empty `agent_name` rendering "Step 2 ()" — are in [#76](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-FE-Stellar/pull/76) and are not money-path findings, so they are not tabled here.

**The same discipline inside the feature PRs.** The hardening pass was the second pass, not the first. [#61](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-BE-Stellar/pull/61) recorded five defects found and fixed during 4.03's own build, including a first version of the refund mutex that could wedge a dispute permanently and a missing migration that would have broken every dispute write on the deployed service; [#63](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-BE-Stellar/pull/63) recorded a cache invalidation that could undo itself; [#65](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-BE-Stellar/pull/65) recorded a released refund claim that carried its dead transaction hash forward, so a buyer's receipt linked a **failed** transaction as the refund in flight.

**Gate on the hardening merge.** Backend: `ruff check`, `ruff format --check` and `mypy` (106 modules) clean; `pytest --cov` **2,177 passed, 2 skipped, 92.53%** against a floor of 82% (2,053 before the pass). Frontend: `npm run lint` and `tsc --noEmit` clean; **1,503 unit tests** across 50 files with coverage 96.99/93.77/95.69/98.75 against gates of 86/78/88/89; **59 e2e** passing; `build` and `bundle` clean, every route inside the 480 KB first-load budget.

**Not fixed, and named as such.** Three items are listed in [#75](https://github.com/Bl0cksmiths/Orizon-Agents-BE-Stellar/pull/75)'s own "Not in this PR": a third settlement state (`charge_confirmed`) so an unconfirmed charge still opens a dispute window without asserting the money moved; `refund_confirmed` on the dispute contract, so the refund is held to the same "the chain vouched for it" rule as the rating — the frontend already reads an explicit `false` as unconfirmed, but the backend does not yet emit it; and `app/services/binding_store.py`, which carries the same unbounded pool timeouts and lifecycle races that were fixed in `dispute_store.py`.

**Reachability, precisely.** None of the above was exposed to a user. The money-path and disclosure defects each need something the deployment cannot provide: the money-path defects need adjudicator credentials behind a refund switch that is off, and the disclosure defects need a dispute to exist, which needs a settlement that is never written. The two live gaps at the top of this document are the reason, and they are also why fixing these was cheap: we were attacking a path that had never carried a user's money, and never will until it is switched on deliberately.
